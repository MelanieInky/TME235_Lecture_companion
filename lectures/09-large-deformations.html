Comparison between small strain and large deformation strain

Compare the linearised strain ε = sym(∇u) with the Green–Lagrange strain E = ½(FTF − I). A rigid rotation is the cleanest test: E stays zero, ε does not.

The deformation drawn is F = R(θ)·[[λ, k],[0, 1]], applied about the square's centre. ε = sym(F − I) and E = ½(FTF − I) are both computed from that same F. Their difference is second order in ∇u, so it is invisible for small deformations and grows quickly once ∇u is not small (including rotation, which ε does not filter out).

Shear rows show the tensor component (ε12), which is half the engineering shear γ12 used in the Voigt vector. The area row is 2D: the ε column is the linearised trace ε11 + ε22, the E column is the exact det F − 1 (in 3D the linearised measure is the full trace εkk).

The dashed shape is what small-strain theory assigns to the current state; it has no rotation, so it can never match a rotated body. The plot is uniaxial only (k = 0, θ = 0) and does not depend on those two sliders; the dots mark the current λ. All three curves agree to first order near λ = 1. The verdict compares ‖E − ε‖/‖E‖ against a 5% threshold. Nothing is clamped: the drawing stays inside its frame over the full slider range.

Polar decomposition

Every deformation gradient with det F > 0 splits as F = R U: first the pure stretch U = √(FTF) along the principal directions Ni, then the rigid rotation R. Set F, then scrub s (or press Play) to watch the two steps. The left figures show how the strain tensors C = FTF and E = ½(C − I) assign a normal strain to every direction in the reference configuration; the probe slider picks one direction.

Order. F = R U is the right (material) polar decomposition: stretch first, in the reference configuration, then rotate. The other order, F = V R, stretches along ni = R Ni after rotating and is not drawn here. From s = 0 to 1 the figure blends I → U linearly and from 1 to 2 it rotates by a fraction of θ. The in-between frames are an illustration; the endpoints are exact.

Left figure. E is a tensor, so it gives a normal strain for every direction. For a material line along N = (cos φ, sin φ) in the reference, E(φ) = N·E N = ½(λ² − 1), where λ is the stretch of that line and λ² = N·C N. The plot shows E(φ) against φ (measured from x₁, counter-clockwise): amber where lines lengthen, blue where they shorten. Its maximum and minimum are the eigenvalues E₁ and E₂, reached along the principal directions N1, N2, which are also principal for U and C. Shear components are not drawn. E is the finite Green–Lagrange strain, not the linearised ε. In the right figure ● marks the material point (1, 1) and the black segment is the probe line carried along by the deformation. x₁ points right, x₂ up.

Limits. Figures are rescaled uniformly when they would leave the frame; “view ×k” says by how much, and the readouts are never rescaled. If λ₁ = λ₂ every direction is principal, and the axes in the right figure are drawn along x₁, x₂. This is 2D: J = det F = λ₁λ₂ is an area ratio. For det F ≤ 0 there is no proper rotation R, so figures and derived tensors are suppressed.
